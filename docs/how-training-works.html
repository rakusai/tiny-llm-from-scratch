How Training Works

We train a small Llama-style language model from scratch on a MacBook with an Apple M4. Llama-style means RMSNorm, grouped-query attention, RoPE and SwiGLU, the design most current LLMs share. This page walks through the whole process with real numbers, from turning text into integers to watching the loss fall and reading the stories the model writes.

2 · Tokenization

Turning text into integers

Models only read numbers, so before training we convert all the text into a sequence of token IDs.

Shakespeare: one character, one token

Each of the 65 distinct characters gets a number, starting from 0. That lookup table, stored in meta.json, is the whole tokenizer.

TinyStories: training our own BPE

BPE stands for Byte Pair Encoding, the standard method used by most LLMs since GPT-2. It starts with the 256 possible byte values and repeatedly merges the most frequent pair of adjacent symbols into a new one. We ran 3,839 merges over the first 200 MB of text. The first three were Ġ+t, h+e and Ġ+a (Ġ marks a space). This is just counting, with no neural network involved.

With only 4,096 entries, only common words get a token of their own. Fittingly for children's stories, emotion words like Ġuncomfortable and Ġadventurous made the cut. We append <|endoftext|> to every story so the model also learns when a story is over.

The .bin format: 2-byte integers, back to back

Each token is stored as a 2-byte integer (uint16, little-endian), one after another, with no header or separators. Training never loads the 1.1 GB file into memory. It memory-maps the file and reads only the slices it needs.

12 00
2f 00
38 00
39 00
3a 00

These are the first 10 bytes of shakespeare/train.bin. Read 0x0012 as 18, 0x002f as 47, and so on, and they spell F i r s t.

3 · One step

What happens in one training step

Training is one step repeated many times: about 530 times in the 3-minute run and 2,000 times for Shakespeare.

Inputs and targets are offset by one

No one has to label anything. The text already holds the answer for every position: whatever token comes next.

inputOnceĠuponĠaĠtime,Ġthere ↓↓↓↓↓↓ targetĠuponĠaĠtime,ĠthereĠwas

The causal mask lets each position see only the tokens before it, so a single window gives 256 questions at once without any peeking.

4 · Loss

Loss: how much probability went to the right answer

Let p be the probability the model assigned to the correct token. The loss is −log p, averaged over every position.

loss = mean( −log p(target) )

A loss of 2.5 means the model is, on average, about as unsure as if it were choosing among 12 equally likely tokens (e2.5 ≈ 12).

Why track training and validation loss separately

We measure the loss both on training data and on held-out validation data the model never learns from. Here is the actual Shakespeare run.

TinyStories is large enough that our runs read only a few percent of it, so the model rarely sees the same text twice. After the 3-minute run, training and validation loss were both 2.50.

5 · Learning rate

Ramp the learning rate up, then bring it down

The learning rate sets how far each update moves the weights. It climbs from 0 to its peak of 0.002 over the first 100 steps (warmup), then follows a cosine curve down to 0.0002. Big steps early sketch the overall shape; small steps late fill in the detail.

If you have a fixed amount of time, plan the schedule to finish within it instead of cutting a longer run short. That is what --time-budget does: it lowers the learning rate according to how much of the time has passed.

Larger models take longer per step, so in a fixed amount of time they get through less data.

A 3-minute showdown

B is about 40% the size of A but got through more than 1.5× as much data, and the two finished essentially tied. The 0.008 gap is within evaluation noise. With three minutes, the advantages of size and of more data cancel out. Give it more compute and the best model size grows roughly with the square root of the compute, the idea behind the Chinchilla scaling law.

Untrained, the model strings together random words. After 2–3 minutes the grammar and dialogue are mostly correct, but the plot drifts ("friends with the ball"). By 10 minutes a classic TinyStories arc shows up (a falling-out, regret, making up, playing together), although the model still mixes up who did what.
